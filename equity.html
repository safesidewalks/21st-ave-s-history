01

Confined here · 1889–1960s

This block wasn't
chosen — it was
what remained

When Beacon Hill was platted in 1889, restrictive covenants were already appearing in Seattle property deeds. The language was explicit: "No person other than the Caucasian race." Beacon Hill, the Central District, and the International District were among the few areas where Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, and Black families could legally purchase homes.

This wasn't a preference. It was a legal constraint. North Seattle, West Seattle, and most of the rest of the city were made unavailable by racial covenants backed by the federal government through FHA lending policies that refused to insure mortgages in integrated neighborhoods — a practice documented in Seattle as early as the 1930s and known as redlining.

The families who settled 21st Ave S and 22nd Ave S in the 1940s, 50s, and 60s were not choosing Beacon Hill over somewhere else. They were choosing Beacon Hill because somewhere else had been systematically closed to them.

In 1967, Sandi's family moved to 21st Ave S. There was no sidewalk. There had never been a sidewalk. There still isn't one today.

"No person other than the Caucasian race."

— Language in Seattle property deeds, documented from 1889 through the 1960s

How the debt was made · 1950s

The annexation
that explains
everything

In 1954, Seattle annexed the area from N 85th St to N 145th St — what is now much of District 5. The annexation brought in new neighborhoods without requiring sidewalk construction. The obligation to build them passed to the City. The City did not follow through. Seventy years later, District 5 has 36% of Seattle's missing sidewalk gaps.

The 2024 Transportation Levy's own whereas clauses acknowledge this explicitly. The City owes District 5 a debt because of what it failed to do at annexation. The logic is sound and the levy honors it — District 5 receives a minimum of 36% of levy sidewalk funds, approximately $40 million.

Now apply the same logic to District 2.

North Beacon Hill was also annexed under unincorporated King County standards that imposed no sidewalk requirements — in the 1950s. The families who moved there had no choice of location. They settled on blocks like 21st Ave S and 22nd Ave S precisely because the neighborhoods receiving sidewalk-promised suburban expansion — the District 5 neighborhoods — were closed to them by racial covenant.

The 1954 annexees received FHA-backed mortgages and new construction in a city that promised them sidewalks. The families on 21st Ave S received none of that. They received a block they were confined to, without sidewalks, and they have been waiting longer than anyone in District 5.

The burden · 1967–present

When the public
system fails,
the cost falls on you

Sandi's family moved to 21st Ave S in 1967. She still lives there. In the decades that followed, her father did what the city would not: he tried to organize the neighbors to fund sidewalk construction themselves.

It didn't work. Not because neighbors were indifferent — but because the money wasn't there. These were working families already paying property taxes into a system that funded sidewalks in other neighborhoods. Being asked to pay again, out of pocket, on top of that, to get what their taxes should have already provided — that was the barrier. The sidewalk didn't get built.

This story is not unusual. Across the country, the pattern is well-documented: when public infrastructure investment is withheld from communities of color, the burden of filling that gap falls on the residents themselves — through self-organized funding drives, through advocacy labor, through decades of emails and meetings and community walks that change nothing.

The families on 21st Ave S have paid property taxes for 40 to 60 years. Those taxes have funded sidewalks in other Seattle neighborhoods — neighborhoods that were, by legal design, more accessible to white families. The infrastructure those taxes built was not shared equitably. The gap that created is what Sandi's father was trying to close with his own money, in his own time, because the public mechanism hadn't arrived.

It still hasn't arrived. What has arrived is a $350,000 asphalt walkway on one side of the street, designed without community input, labeled internally as "Low Cost Sidewalks," and counted as delivery.

Dan Ostrowski, a current resident, wrote to SDOT in July 2025 noting the block's history of "self-funded drainage fixes" — another instance of the same pattern. When the public system doesn't show up, residents pay twice: once in taxes, and once out of pocket.

The levy · 2024

The framework
built to identify
exactly this

The 2024 Transportation Levy is not a neutral infrastructure program. Its equity framework was explicitly designed to identify and correct the kind of long-term, racially rooted disinvestment that defines Beacon Hill's sidewalk history.

SDOT's own prioritization system scores every missing sidewalk segment on a 24-point scale combining pedestrian danger, connectivity, and equity. 21st Ave S scores 12 out of 12 on equity — the maximum possible, and 20 out of 24 overall — placing it in the top 7.8% of all 11,550 missing sidewalk segments citywide. Watch what the levy does with that.

The levy's equity framework was designed to score exactly what it found on 21st Ave S: deep need, long wait, high danger, maximum equity score. These 100 squares — $111 million — are the fund the levy created to start closing gaps like this one.

The levy's own whereas clauses invoke the logic of historical debt: communities that were promised infrastructure and never received it are owed a proportional repayment. Scroll to see how the squares are divided.

Three districts. Three floors. The mosaic already tells you something.

The levy sets minimum funding floors for three council districts. The squares are now colored by district. All 100 are in the same grid.

There is a lot of blue. District 5, in north Seattle. There is very little red. District 2, which includes Beacon Hill, the Rainier Valley, Columbia City, Othello — the communities whose families were confined here by racial covenant while the blue squares' neighborhoods were being built for white families with FHA-backed mortgages.

The gray squares are unspecified — no district floor. They could go anywhere in the city.

36 squares for District 5. 17 for District 2.

The squares are separating into their groups. District 5 gets 36 — approximately $40 million. The levy's whereas clauses explain this directly: the 1954 annexation of North Seattle created a sidewalk gap, and the city owes a debt. That logic is sound. The levy honors it.

District 2 gets 17 squares — approximately $19 million. The levy's whereas clauses do not name the reason District 2's floor exists. The reason is older than 1954, harder to acknowledge, and more damaging. District 2's families were here before District 5 was annexed. They were here because everywhere else was legally closed to them.

District 5 waited 72 years. District 2 has waited 120 to 143.

These 17 squares carry the full weight of District 2's sidewalk debt.

District 2 accounts for 56% of all fatal and serious pedestrian injury crashes citywide — SDOT's own Vision Zero figure. Its equity score is 12 out of 12. The maximum. Its funding floor is the lowest named minimum of the three districts that receive one.

The framework identifies the debt. The formula refuses to pay it proportionally.

The 21st Ave S asphalt walkway costs $350,000. That is 31.5% of one square. Less than one third of one of District 2's already-inadequate 17.

It was designed without community input. One side only. Asphalt. Labeled internally as "Low Cost Sidewalks." And because of the Chair's Amendment that renamed "New Sidewalks" to "New Sidewalks and Sidewalk Alternatives," it counts as levy delivery. A fraction of a fraction. Marked as done.

The levy rewards the neighborhood whose sidewalk debt is newer. It underfunds the neighborhood whose sidewalk debt was created by racial exclusion.

The levy's equity framework scores 21st Ave S at the top of its own priority list. Its funding formula then directs less money to the district that contains that block than to any other named district. The framework identifies the debt. The formula refuses to pay it.

And the delivery mechanism — "sidewalk alternatives" — ensures that even the 17% minimum can be met with asphalt paths on one side of residential streets. The community that waited longest, scored highest on danger, and received a perfect equity score gets the lowest funding floor and the weakest delivery standard.

06

The ask · May 2026

Not a subsidy.
A debt.

In February 2026, 58 residents of 21st Ave S were surveyed. Not one preferred the planned asphalt walkway. 86% wanted permanent concrete sidewalks on both sides. 88% feel unsafe or very unsafe walking the street today.

Kashina's family decided not to rent on the block because her child would have to walk in the travel lane to reach a school bus. Letha says walking this street feels like "a matter of when a pedestrian will be hit, not if." Four residents use wheelchairs or assistive devices. Twenty-six families walk with children or strollers.

There is a precedent that worries this community. S Kenyon St in South Beacon Hill received a similar asphalt quick-build in 2021. It is already deteriorating. Wheel stops are being swallowed by weeds. The effective walkway width may fall short of ADA standards. An asphalt alternative does not age like concrete. It does not support tree canopy the same way. It does not manage stormwater the same way. It is, as neighbor Meaghan — a landscape architect — has documented, a fundamentally different category of infrastructure presented as equivalent delivery.

Sandi's father tried to organize neighbors to fund what the city owed this community. The barrier was financial — working families already paying taxes into a system that built sidewalks elsewhere, being asked to pay again out of pocket for infrastructure those taxes should have covered. It didn't work. The 2024 Transportation Levy was supposed to be the mechanism that made that impossible burden unnecessary. Instead it created a framework that identifies this community's need perfectly — maximum equity score, top 7.8% of all missing sidewalk segments citywide — and then directs the minimum possible resources to address it, through the weakest permissible delivery standard.

The families on 21st Ave S have paid taxes for 40 to 60 years. This is not a request for a subsidy. It is a request that a debt be paid.

"This is not a subsidy. It is a debt."

— Community framing, 21st Ave S, 2026

What paying the debt looks like

From SDOT: A written answer — does the asphalt walkway permanently close the pedestrian infrastructure need for this block, or does it remain on the unfinished list?

From CM Eddie Lin: A formal request to SDOT for that written answer, and a commitment that 21st Ave S stays on the unfinished business list — not marked complete — so permanent concrete sidewalks on both sides remain a reachable goal.

From the levy: Recognition that "sidewalk alternatives" in historically redlined neighborhoods with perfect equity scores are not equivalent delivery — and that the funding floor for District 2 should reflect what the equity framework actually identifies.
