Redlining · 1889–1954

Seattle’s neighborhoods were not shaped by preference. By 1889, property deeds named who could live where — most of the city legally closed to Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, Black, and Jewish families. Beacon Hill was not.

The neighborhoods that were off-limits were being built out in the 1950s with FHA-backed mortgages and promised sidewalks. The families on 21st Ave S received neither. In 1967, Sandi’s family moved here. There was no sidewalk. There had never been one. There still isn’t one today.

Neighbor record · 1967–present

In the decades after her family moved in, Sandi’s father did what the city would not: he tried to organize the neighbors to fund sidewalk construction themselves. He couldn’t get enough buy-in. The sidewalk didn’t get built.

When public infrastructure investment is withheld from communities of color, the burden of filling that gap falls on residents themselves — through self-organized funding drives, advocacy labor, and decades of effort that change nothing. The families on 21st Ave S have paid property taxes for 40 to 60 years. Those taxes funded sidewalks in other Seattle neighborhoods — neighborhoods that were, by legal design, more accessible to white families.

Without a sidewalk · 1967–present

What the gap costs

The racially restrictive covenants are gone. What they produced is not.

When infrastructure investment is withheld from a neighborhood — by law, then by policy, then by inertia — the gap doesn’t stay abstract. It becomes a daily calculation. Which side of the street is safer today. Whether to let your child walk to the bus stop alone. What to do about the water pooling in your driveway because the drainage was never built right.

The families on 21st Ave S have been running those calculations for decades.

In 2015, Lauren borrowed a radar gun from SDOT, collected speed data on 21st Ave S herself — a city requirement before SDOT would conduct its own study — and turned in the results. A year later, having heard nothing, she wrote back asking when SDOT would conduct its follow-up study. There is no record anyone followed up. SDOT’s own study came seven years later: 77% of drivers on S College St exceed the 25 mph speed limit. The 85th percentile speed: 32.7 mph.

“The firefighters are currently performing a rescue extrication. This is the intersection my neighbors and I cross every day taking our kids to and from school.”

Letha, email to SDOT — June 6, 2023

In February 2020, a Beacon Hill Elementary student and her mother were struck by a truck at 22nd Ave S and S College St. In June 2023, Letha watched a post-collision rescue extrication at the same intersection and emailed SDOT that day. The following month, multiple collisions were reported at the same intersection.

Meanwhile, neighbors have managed their own drainage for decades. In the 1990s, several households pooled their own money to build asphalt berms to keep stormwater from flooding their homes — a problem that had gone unaddressed for years. A resident who has lived on the block since the 1980s still remembers it. Calvin, whose parents owned the home before him, still deals with uncontrolled runoff from a city storm drain in front of the property today.

21st & 22nd Ave S · 2021–2026

The Neighbors Who
Pushed Back

“The lack of a compliant, contiguous path of travel is a clear violation of the ADA… renders me a prisoner in my own home.”

Katt, resident — 21st Ave S

To understand what is happening on 21st Ave S today, you have to look one block east first. 22nd Ave S is the cautionary tale. Residents there spent years doing what the system asked: filing reports after crashes, requesting speed studies, tracking down timelines. They were told a sidewalk was coming. It wasn’t.

22nd Ave S — What happened one block east

SDOT tells Letha a concrete sidewalk is coming to 22nd Ave S — before Judkins Park Station opens.

One block west, on 21st Ave S, the pattern repeated — but compressed. There was no promise to break. The city never proposed a permanent sidewalk at all. It went straight to asphalt. And it didn’t tell the neighbors until after the scope was signed.

21st Ave S — What happened here
Aug 4–7, 2023
SDOT signs the project scope: asphalt path, west side only.
Aug 11, 2023
Community walk-through — requested by residents to give input on the plan.

The neighbors showed up. The decision had already been made.

In both cases, residents were left to be the connective tissue between siloed city departments that weren’t talking to each other. On 22nd, they spent years as messengers — relaying crash reports, chasing timelines — only to watch the project evaporate. On 21st, they didn’t even get that far. So they took matters into their own hands.

The Gap · 2022–2026

What was promised.
What is being built.

In 2022, City Council passed a budget with a $2 million proviso directed toward missing sidewalks in District 2 — with 22nd Ave S named first. Councilmember Morales secured it. Residents on 22nd were not told. When SDOT cancelled the 22nd Ave S project in 2023, the proviso appears to have been redirected — toward the cheap asphalt plan on 21st Ave S. No one told the residents on either block. The legal basis for that redirect was never answered in writing.

SDOT’s internal label for this project is “Low Cost Sidewalks.” The community has not called it that.

There is a nearby precedent. S Kenyon St received a similar asphalt quick-build in 2021. Four years later, it is already deteriorating — wheel stops swallowed by weeds, drainage unresolved. The community has documented it.

Once asphalt is installed, does SDOT consider the block’s pedestrian infrastructure need permanently solved?

SDOT project lead Madeline McCort was asked this directly in writing on April 29, 2026. The question was not answered.

The Funding · 2024

$111 million for new sidewalks.
Not all districts are equal.

In November 2024, Seattle voters approved a $1.55 billion transportation levy. Of that, $111 million is designated for new sidewalks over eight years. Each council district received a minimum funding floor — and those floors are not equal.

District 5’s higher floor reflects a 1954 annexation commitment — North Seattle neighborhoods were promised sidewalks when they joined the city. District 2 received no such promise. Racial restrictive covenants had already decided who could live there.

The families on 21st Ave S could not move to those North Seattle neighborhoods. Covenants barred them. Now, decades later, those same neighborhoods receive two dollars of levy funding for every one dollar that goes to District 2 — justified by a promise the families on 21st Ave S were never allowed to benefit from.
