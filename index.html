Redlining · 1889–1954

Seattle’s neighborhoods were not shaped by preference. By 1889, property deeds named who could live where — most of the city legally closed to Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, Black, and Jewish families. Beacon Hill was not.

The neighborhoods that were off-limits were being built out in the 1950s with FHA-backed mortgages and promised sidewalks. The families on 21st Ave S received neither. In 1967, Sandi’s family moved here. There was no sidewalk. There had never been one. There still isn’t one today.

Neighbor record · 1967–present

In the decades after her family moved in, Sandi’s father did what the city would not: he tried to organize the neighbors to fund sidewalk construction themselves. He couldn’t get enough buy-in. The sidewalk didn’t get built.

When public infrastructure investment is withheld from communities of color, the burden of filling that gap falls on residents themselves — through self-organized funding drives, advocacy labor, and decades of effort that change nothing. The families on 21st Ave S have paid property taxes for 40 to 60 years. Those taxes funded sidewalks in other Seattle neighborhoods — neighborhoods that were, by legal design, more accessible to white families.

Without a sidewalk · 1967–present

What the gap costs

The racially restrictive covenants are gone. What they produced is not.

When infrastructure investment is withheld from a neighborhood — by law, then by policy, then by inertia — the gap doesn’t stay abstract. It becomes a daily calculation. Which side of the street is safer today. Whether to let your child walk to the bus stop alone. What to do about the water pooling in your driveway because the drainage was never built right.

The families on 21st Ave S have been running those calculations for decades.

In 2015, Lauren borrowed a radar gun from SDOT, collected speed data on 21st Ave S herself — a city requirement before SDOT would conduct its own study — and turned in the results. A year later, having heard nothing, she wrote back asking when SDOT would conduct its follow-up study. There is no record anyone followed up. SDOT’s own study came seven years later: 77% of drivers on S College St exceed the 25 mph speed limit. The 85th percentile speed: 32.7 mph. Seven years after Lauren turned in her data, SDOT confirmed what she had already measured.

“The firefighters are currently performing a rescue extrication. This is the intersection my neighbors and I cross every day taking our kids to and from school.”

22nd & 21st Ave S · 2021–2023

The Community That
Kept the Record

To understand what is being built on 21st Ave S, you have to start one block east.

22nd Ave S runs north toward Rainier Ave S, one of the neighborhood’s main arterials. Without sidewalks, the roadway is effectively wider than it needs to be, and drivers treat it accordingly. In the words of a resident who wrote to SDOT in July 2023: “Drivers treat 22nd Ave S as an overflow street when the pedestrians-be-damned arterial of Rainier is busy and alternative route 23rd Ave is busy. … The very grade that makes sidewalk installation challenging also makes drivers go too fast.”

Parents crossing with children in strollers stepped off the curb into moving traffic. Residents coming in and out of their driveways shared the same narrow lane with cars at speed.

Letha had been documenting this for over a decade — submitting crash reports, writing to the city, asking for infrastructure. In February 2020, a classmate of her daughter’s was struck by a truck turning from eastbound College onto northbound 22nd Ave S. The mother and child were heading west on College and the truck didn’t see them coming. A totaled car, a broken arm, an emergency room visit at the start of a pandemic. Her neighbor Rachel was making that same crossing with her own child every day.

When SDOT’s email arrived in October 2021 promising a concrete sidewalk, they had reason to believe it.

SDOT project manager Nicole Willis emails a resident directly with a written promise: the concrete sidewalk on 22nd Ave S will be complete “by the time the new Judkins Park sound transit station opens in spring of 2023.” That station opens in April 2023. No sidewalk is built.

Included: CM Morales’ Council Budget Action SDOT-105-B-001 — $2 million of REET I funds added to SDOT’s Pedestrian Master Plan – New Sidewalks project. The Council’s stated intent is unambiguous: 22nd Ave S — between S College St and S Bayview St — is named first priority. SDOT will later argue the proviso doesn’t require it. Nearly four years after the written promise, SDOT circulates 90% design plans — still only for the “21st Ave S Low-Cost Walkway project”: a “new 6–8′ asphalt walkway with pre-cast concrete wheel stops.” Not a sidewalk. Still not built. The downgrade chain is complete: sidewalk → walkway → paused walkway → 90% plans.

CM Morales’ July 31, 2023 newsletter — explicitly names 22nd Ave S as the reason she secured the $2 million proviso. Four days earlier, her own policy director had emailed a neighbor to ask whether the sidewalks had been installed. Her office had not been told the funds were redirected.

The cancellation was not the end of the story. It was a substitution.

Rachel had asked Burgesser to walk the street on July 20 — the morning she was almost struck from her own driveway. He didn’t reply for 15 days. On August 4, he agreed to schedule a walk and wrote that he expected to have “new information to share on safety improvements we’re planning for this area as soon as next week.”

He was already holding it.

Between August 4 and 7, 2023, SDOT’s Project Initiation Summary for 21st Ave S was signed by four reviewers. Scope: a 6-foot asphalt walkway with precast concrete wheel stops, west side only, $350,000. In SDOT’s internal finance system, the project was entered under the name “Low-Cost Walkway,” as documented in SDOT’s own project records obtained through a May 2026 public records request. That label never appeared in any materials shared with residents.

On August 7, Burgesser proposed August 11. Rachel said yes.

On August 11, Letha and Rachel brought SDOT officials to 22nd Ave S. They walked the intersection where a child had been struck three years earlier, where CM Morales’s car had been hit eleven days before. They showed them what was still missing.

What they didn’t know, what neither of them had been told, was that the scope for 21st Ave S had been signed before Burgesser proposed the meeting.

They were not being consulted. They were being managed.

Strategic management — what it looks like in practice

Strategic management is distinct from responsiveness. It is the use of the forms of engagement — replies, meetings, walks, letters — to satisfy the appearance of a process while the outcome is predetermined. Every communication arrives on time. Every meeting is scheduled. Every question receives an answer — except the ones that matter. The record shows not a failure of process, but a process working exactly as intended: to absorb community concern without altering the result.

“Hopefully we do not need to wait for another city councilmember, or another child, to be injured at 22nd and College before action is taken.”

— Rachel & Letha, letter to SDOT Director Spotts, September 19, 2023

21st Ave S · 2023–2026

What $350,000 Buys

The community’s first official notice of any project on 21st Ave S arrived in May 2025 — a construction notice, printed in five languages, announcing what had been decided.

The scope had been locked 22 months earlier.

What $350,000 builds: a 6-foot asphalt path on the west side of the street only. Precast concrete wheel stops in place of curbs. No concrete. No coverage on the east side, where many students approach from Rainier Ave.

Internally, SDOT calls this project “Low-Cost Walkway.” The community has not called it that.

There is a nearby precedent that worries residents. S Kenyon St in South Beacon Hill received a similar asphalt quick-build in 2021. It is already deteriorating. Wheel stops are being swallowed by weeds.

By SDOT’s own prioritization data, this block is Tier 1, the highest possible. Equity score: 12 out of 12, the maximum. Among 11,550 missing sidewalk segments citywide, 21st Ave S ranks in the top 7.8%. An asphalt path on one side of the street is not the standard the rest of Seattle is held to.

Once this project is complete, will SDOT consider the pedestrian infrastructure need for this block permanently solved?

SDOT project lead Madeline McCort was asked this directly in writing on April 29, 2026. The question was not answered.

The same question had appeared internally a year earlier — flagged by SDOT’s own design reviewers in comments on the 90% engineering plans, summer 2025. There is no record of a response.

The Funding · 2024

$111 million for new sidewalks.
Not all districts are equal.

In November 2024, Seattle voters approved a $1.55 billion transportation levy. Of that, $111 million is designated for new sidewalks over eight years. Each council district received a minimum funding floor — and those floors are not equal.

District 5’s higher floor reflects a 1954 annexation commitment — North Seattle neighborhoods were promised sidewalks when they joined the city. District 2 received no such promise. Racial restrictive covenants had already decided who could live there.

The families on 21st Ave S could not move to those North Seattle neighborhoods. Covenants barred them. Now, decades later, those same neighborhoods receive two dollars of levy funding for every one dollar that goes to District 2 — justified by a promise the families on 21st Ave S were never allowed to benefit from.
