How long is too long to wait for sidewalks?

Redlining · 1889–1968

“No person other than
the Caucasian race.”

Seattle’s neighborhoods were not shaped by preference. By 1889, property deeds were already naming who could live where, and most of the city was legally closed to Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, Black, and Jewish families. Beacon Hill was one of the few places left open to them.

The covenants were not a brief experiment. They held for generations, and the city defended them. By the early 1960s, other states had begun outlawing housing discrimination; the first did so in 1959. Seattle had the same chance in 1964 and voted it down, rejecting an open-housing ordinance by nearly two to one. The change came only when the federal government forced it. The Fair Housing Act of 1968 barred discrimination in the sale, rental, and financing of housing on the basis of race, color, religion, and national origin.

The law could not undo the culture it inherited. When families of color moved into whiter neighborhoods, many met harassment and threats meant to drive them out. The covenants were dead on paper, but what they had taught the city outlived them.

Money and concrete followed the same lines the deeds had drawn. Through the 1950s, the neighborhoods that had been off-limits filled in with FHA-backed mortgages and new sidewalks. The families on 21st Ave S got neither.

Neighbor record · 1967–present

In 1967, Sandi’s family moved here. There was no sidewalk. There had never been one. There still isn’t one today.

In the decades after her family moved in, Sandi’s father did what the city would not: he tried to organize the neighbors to fund sidewalk construction themselves. He couldn’t get enough buy-in. The sidewalk didn’t get built.

When public infrastructure investment is withheld from communities of color, the burden of filling that gap falls on residents themselves: self-organized funding drives, advocacy labor, decades of effort that changed nothing. The families on 21st Ave S have paid property taxes for 40 to 60 years. Those taxes built sidewalks in other Seattle neighborhoods, the ones that were, by legal design, more open to white families.

The city, to its credit, has not ignored this history. Its plans say redlining out loud; its maps rank this neighborhood by displacement risk; its Anti-Displacement Action Plan sorts the response into four tidy pillars: Plan, Produce, Protect, Preserve. The vocabulary is fluent, well-staffed, and aimed squarely at neighborhoods like this one. The harm has been named with real precision. What never arrived on 21st Ave S were curb-and-gutter concrete sidewalks.

How much has Seattle spent studying, naming, and apologizing for this inequity, next to what it has spent actually ending it?

Without a sidewalk · 1967–present

What the gap costs

The racially restrictive covenants are gone. What they produced is not.

When infrastructure investment is withheld from a neighborhood — by law, then by policy, then by inertia — the gap doesn’t stay abstract. It becomes a daily calculation. Which side of the street is safer today. Whether to let your child walk to the bus stop alone. What to do about the water pooling in your driveway because the drainage was never built right.

The families on 21st Ave S have been running those calculations for decades.

In 2015, Lauren borrowed a radar gun from SDOT, collected speed data on 21st Ave S herself — a city requirement before SDOT would conduct its own study — and turned in the results. A year later, having heard nothing, she wrote back asking when SDOT would conduct its follow-up study. There is no record anyone followed up. SDOT’s own study came seven years later: 77% of drivers on S College St exceed the 25 mph speed limit. The 85th percentile speed: 32.7 mph. Seven years after Lauren turned in her data, SDOT confirmed what she had already measured.

22nd & 21st Ave S · 2021–2023

The Community That
Kept the Record

To understand what is being built on 21st Ave S, you have to start one block east.

22nd Ave S runs north toward Rainier Ave S, one of the neighborhood’s main arterials. Without sidewalks, the roadway is effectively wider than it needs to be, and drivers treat it accordingly. In the words of a resident who wrote to SDOT in July 2023: “Drivers treat 22nd Ave S as an overflow street when the pedestrians-be-damned arterial of Rainier is busy and alternative route 23rd Ave is busy. … The very grade that makes sidewalk installation challenging also makes drivers go too fast.”

Parents crossing with children in strollers stepped off the curb into moving traffic. Residents coming in and out of their driveways shared the same narrow lane with cars at speed.

Letha had spent more than a decade documenting this, filing crash reports and writing to a city that didn’t act. In February 2020, a classmate of her daughter’s was struck by a truck turning from eastbound College onto northbound 22nd Ave S. The mother and child were heading west on College and the truck didn’t see them coming. A totaled car, a broken arm, an emergency room visit at the start of a pandemic. Her neighbor Rachel was making that same crossing with her own child every day.

“

I am pleased to let you know that we are in the planning stages of building a new sidewalk on the west side of 22nd Ave S from Walker to Bayview! We are planning to have it complete by the time of the new Judkins Park sound transit station opens in spring of 2023.

Nicole Willis, SDOT Project Manager — October 15, 2021

When SDOT’s email arrived in October 2021 promising a concrete sidewalk, they had reason to believe it.

So they eased off. The October 2021 email had tied the sidewalk to a date: finished by the time the Judkins Park light rail station opened, then expected in spring 2023. When the station’s opening slipped, residents assumed the sidewalk had slipped with it. The delay looked ordinary, a small project waiting on a bigger one. They had a written promise, and no reason to keep pressing the city for it.

The promise didn’t hold. On June 6, 2023, a resident wrote SDOT to check on the timeline, days after watching a rescue crew cut someone out of a car at 22nd and College. Burgesser took twenty-seven days to answer. When the reply came, the project was cancelled: the grades were too steep, the cost too high. He never mentioned the $2 million the Council had set aside for it, or where the money had gone.

The money behind that promise had a name on it. In the 2022 city budget, Councilmember Morales secured a $2 million proviso that named 22nd Ave S as the first street to get sidewalks. By the summer of 2023 the project was dead, and her own office learned it from a neighbor rather than from SDOT. Around the same time, her car was struck at 22nd and College, the intersection the proviso was meant to fix.

“

Hopefully we do not need to wait for another city councilmember, or another child, to be injured at 22nd and College before action is taken.

Rachel & Letha, letter to SDOT Director Spotts, September 19, 2023

CM Morales’ July 31, 2023 newsletter explicitly names 22nd Ave S as the reason she secured the $2 million proviso. Four days earlier, her own policy director had emailed a neighbor to ask whether the sidewalks had been installed. Her office had not been told the funds were redirected.

The cancellation was not the end of the story. It was a substitution.

Rachel had asked Burgesser to walk the street on July 20, the morning she was almost struck from her own driveway. He didn’t reply for 15 days. On August 4, he agreed to schedule a walk and wrote that he expected to have “new information to share on safety improvements we’re planning for this area as soon as next week.”

The information already existed.

Between August 4 and 7, 2023, SDOT’s Project Initiation Summary for 21st Ave S was signed by four reviewers. Scope: a 6-foot asphalt walkway with precast concrete wheel stops, west side only, $350,000. In SDOT’s internal finance system, the project was entered under the name “Low-Cost Walkway,” as documented in SDOT’s own project records obtained through a May 2026 public records request. That label never appeared in any materials shared with residents.

On August 7, Burgesser proposed August 11. Rachel said yes.

On August 11, Letha and Rachel brought SDOT officials to 22nd Ave S. They walked the intersection where a child had been struck three years earlier, where CM Morales’s car had been hit eleven days before. They showed them what was still missing.

What they didn’t know, what neither of them had been told, was that the scope for 21st Ave S had been signed before Burgesser proposed the meeting.

They were not being consulted. They were being managed.

Strategic management: what it looks like in practice

Strategic management is distinct from responsiveness. It uses the visible forms of engagement, the replies and meetings and walks and letters, to satisfy the appearance of process while the outcome stays fixed. Every communication arrives on time. Every meeting is scheduled. Every question gets an answer, except the ones that matter. The record shows not a failure of process, but a process working exactly as intended: to absorb community concern without altering the result.

21st Ave S · 2023–2026

What $350,000 Buys

Residents asked first. In January 2023, neighbors near 21st Ave S and S College St contacted SDOT about sidewalk improvements. An SDOT planner logged their information and decided to wait: the agency would reach out, he wrote, once it was “ready to share information about the project.”

The community’s first official notice came more than two years later, in May 2025: a construction notice, printed in five languages, announcing what had already been decided.

The scope had been locked 22 months earlier.

What $350,000 builds: a 6-foot asphalt path on the west side of the street only. Precast concrete wheel stops in place of curbs. No concrete. No coverage on the east side, where many students approach from Rainier Ave.

SDOT explicitly does not call it a sidewalk. In its own records the project is a “Low-Cost Walkway,” an asphalt path with wheel stops rather than the concrete sidewalk the 2022 proviso named. The agency had already judged a real concrete sidewalk on 22nd Ave S too expensive, calling its own $1.5 million estimate too large a share of the proviso for one project. Asphalt cost less per block, so asphalt is what the proviso money bought.

Residents point to a nearby precedent. S Kenyon St in South Beacon Hill got the same kind of asphalt quick-build in 2016, and it’s already failing. The wheel stops are disappearing into the weeds.

The Funding · 2024

$111 million for new sidewalks.
Not all districts are equal.

In November 2024, Seattle voters approved a $1.55 billion transportation levy. Of that, $111 million is designated for new sidewalks over eight years. Each council district received a minimum funding floor. Those floors are not equal.

Minimum levy floor by district

District 5’s higher floor reflects a 1954 annexation commitment. North Seattle neighborhoods were promised sidewalks when they joined the city. District 2 received no such promise.

The families on 21st Ave S could not move to those North Seattle neighborhoods. Covenants barred them by deed. Now, decades later, those same neighborhoods receive two dollars of levy funding for every one dollar that goes to District 2. District 5 has more missing sidewalk than District 2, so a larger share might look fair. It isn’t. Per stretch of missing sidewalk, District 5 still draws about $10,300 in levy money to District 2’s $7,300, nearly 40% more. The reason is a 1954 promise made to neighborhoods that had legally locked these families out, a promise they were never allowed to benefit from.

Beyond Four Blocks · 2026

These are four blocks — two on 21st Ave S, two on 22nd Ave S — and they sit inside a citywide pattern of disinvestment. Across south Seattle, the streets still missing sidewalks line up with the areas the city redlined in the 1930s. What makes 21st and 22nd Ave S worth a closer look is that the paper trail survived. The budget line names the street. A written promise went to a resident. An internal SDOT email later walked it back.

The city has never answered the question that record raises. The Council named this street in the 2022 budget and set aside $2 million for it. By 2026 that money is no longer in the project’s budget, and no one at the city has explained where it went. The councilmember who wrote the proviso backed it. Four years later it still isn’t built. So what is happening on the blocks where no one kept the emails?
