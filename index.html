North Beacon Hill · Seattle · 2019–2026

Two streets,
one story

How Seattle strung along a neighborhood it had already written off.

The emails always came. That is what you have to understand first. When residents of North Beacon Hill wrote to the Seattle Department of Transportation to ask about the sidewalks their children needed to walk safely to school, the responses arrived promptly. They were professional. They were warm. They expressed, with evident sincerity, that safety was a top priority.

For four years, the emails came. The sidewalks did not.

What the paper trail shows — 584 pages of internal communications released under public records request, engineering plan sets, and City Council legislation obtained and reviewed for this account — is something more unsettling than neglect. It is management: a system in which every letter is answered and no promise is kept, in which community engagement is scheduled after decisions are already made, in which the appearance of responsiveness is maintained while the outcome is never seriously in doubt.

The neighborhood at the center of this story is historically redlined. The families on these two adjacent blocks in North Beacon Hill are predominantly multigenerational Chinese immigrants who have lived here for 40 to 60 years. They have paid property taxes. They have watched other streets in other neighborhoods — whiter neighborhoods, wealthier neighborhoods — receive the permanent concrete infrastructure that their blocks have been promised and denied in rotating cycles for decades.

In Seattle's own accounting, District 2 — which includes Beacon Hill — accounted for 56% of all fatal and serious pedestrian injury crashes citywide in 2021. SDOT's Vision Zero review attributed this directly to "the geometry of our streets." These families live inside that statistic. What the city offered them, after years of documentation and advocacy, was an asphalt path.

This is the story of two streets — and how one became a substitute for the other.

Part One · 2021–2023

22nd Ave S:
The promise and what happened to it

A note on terms
"Strategic management" — what it looks like in practice

Strategic management is distinct from responsiveness. It is the use of the forms of engagement — replies, meetings, walks, letters — to satisfy the appearance of a process while the outcome is predetermined. Every communication arrives on time. Every meeting is scheduled. Every question receives an answer — except the ones that matter. The record shows not a failure of process, but a process working exactly as intended: to absorb community concern without altering the result.

In October 2021, a resident named Letha received an email from Nicole Willis, an SDOT project manager. The news was good: SDOT was planning a new sidewalk on the west side of 22nd Ave S from Walker to Bayview, to be completed before the Judkins Park light rail station opened in spring 2023. Letha had been advocating for safe streets near her children's schools for over a decade. Her neighbor Rachel was making the same dangerous crossing with her own child every day.

They waited. The light rail opening was delayed. They assumed the sidewalk delay was related.

They were wrong about that. The sidewalk was not delayed. It had been quietly cancelled — and no one had told them.

In the 2022 budget cycle, Seattle City Council member Tammy Morales had secured a budget proviso — a legislative condition restricting how appropriated funds must be spent — that directed $2 million toward new sidewalk infrastructure in District 2. The budget action named 22nd Ave S between S College St and S Bayview St as the first priority location. It was not a suggestion. It was a Council directive backed by explicit restrictions on the use of the funds.

SDOT had determined, internally, that 22nd Ave S alone would cost at least $1.5 million. That was too large a share of the proviso for a single project. The funds were redirected to deliver more blocks of cheaper infrastructure in other locations. The community was not informed. The Council was not asked. The proviso's named beneficiary simply ceased to exist in SDOT's work plan, and nothing took its place on 22nd Ave S.

The first indication Letha had that anything was wrong came on June 6, 2023, when she wrote to SDOT after witnessing a rescue extrication at 22nd Ave S and S College St — the intersection her children cross every school day. She was checking on the timeline. On July 3, SDOT project manager David Burgesser replied: the 22nd Ave S project was cancelled. Grades too steep. Too expensive. SDOT was "evaluating" alternatives.

He did not mention the $2 million. He did not mention the proviso. He did not explain where the Council-directed funds had gone.

What followed over the next six weeks was a documented cascade of near-misses and collisions on the very streets the proviso was meant to protect. On July 20, Letha reported another collision at 22nd/College involving several children. That same morning, she and her son had nearly been struck while crossing S College St — a westbound driver speeding past a fresh wreck and the line of cars backed up behind it. Rachel emailed separately: she had almost been hit by the same van from her own driveway, on 22nd Ave S between Bayview and College.

One week later, CM Morales's own car was struck by a speeding vehicle at S College St and 22nd Ave S. Her policy director Devin Silvernail, upon learning that the proviso sidewalk had never been built, added SDOT Director Greg Spotts to the email thread and wrote that he worried the funds had been "either left unspent at the time by the department or used on a different project."

He was right on the second count. No one had told him.

On September 19, 2023, Rachel sent a formal accountability letter to Director Spotts, copying elected officials. She asked five specific questions: where had the $2 million gone, why had it not funded 22nd Ave S, what was the legal basis for not following the Council's allocation, what barriers remained to building the sidewalk, and what steps could the community take?

A response arrived. It was thorough. It was signed by a named official. It addressed four of the five questions.

The legal basis question — question (c), the one that went directly to whether SDOT had acted within its authority when it redirected Council-directed funds — was not answered. It has never been answered.

What the internal emails show is the machinery that produced that carefully bounded response. SDOT Council liaison Bill LaBorde forwarded Rachel's letter internally with a note apologizing for her "tone" and advising that the proviso's named locations "were not line item appropriations." Burgesser drafted a reply. Deputy Division Director Brian Dougherty was copied. LaBorde signed off: "Looks great to me." The response was sent the following morning.

Three questions answered. One deflected. The community thanked for their patience.

"Hopefully we do not need to wait for another city councilmember, or another child, to be injured at 22nd and College before action is taken."

— Rachel & Letha, letter to SDOT Director Spotts, Sep 19, 2023
SDOT had already decided where the community's money would go. It would go to a different street.

Part Two · 2023–2026

21st Ave S:
The substitute and what it cost

Between August 4 and August 7, 2023 — three days before Burgesser emailed Rachel to schedule the community walk she had been requesting for weeks — SDOT's Project Initiation Summary for 21st Ave S was signed by four reviewers. The scope was locked: a 6-foot asphalt walkway with precast concrete wheel stops, on the west side of 21st Ave S only, for $350,000. In SDOT's internal finance system, the project was entered under the name "Low Cost Sidewalks." That label never appeared in any materials shared with residents.

The community walk happened on August 11. Residents showed city officials the dangerous conditions on 22nd Ave S and S College St. They made their case. The scope was already locked. The walk changed nothing.

The community's first official notification of any project on 21st Ave S came in May 2025 — a construction notice in the mail, in five languages, announcing what had been decided.

SDOT's own engineers, meanwhile, had found problems the community was never told about.

When 30% design plans were submitted in December 2023, the agency's ADA reviewer found that driveway cross-slopes exceeded federal accessibility limits throughout the entire block, and that ramp slopes had not been specified at all. Urban Forestry reviewer Katey Bean noted the tree planting detail would place trees in conditions where they were "very likely to die within a few years." In June 2024, the project was paused because a topographic survey was needed for ADA compliance — and levy rules prohibited using current levy funds for work not delivered within the levy period. SDOT had to locate approximately $5,000 in separate funds just to continue designing the project.

At the 90% stage in the summer of 2025, Katey Bean flagged the tree problem again — for the third time. A stormwater reviewer asked whether the shallow swales could handle the water flow the project would generate. A third reviewer asked in writing why the design used only paint and flexible delineator posts — not a physical barrier — to separate pedestrians from traffic where people would stand waiting to cross S College St. A fourth reviewer asked whether the design at the far end of the block would permanently foreclose the possibility of building a compliant sidewalk on the east side of the street.

None of these questions appeared in the Q&A letter SDOT sent to residents in April 2026. That letter answered several technical questions. It did not answer the one the community has been asking since 2021: once this project is complete, will SDOT consider the pedestrian infrastructure need for this block permanently solved?

The engineering record suggests SDOT's own reviewers were not sure of the answer either. They asked it themselves, in the margins of the plans, and no one wrote back.

"Why terminate the wheelstops before the area where pedestrians will be standing? It's just paint and posts that a person can knock down."

— SDOT reviewer comment, 90% plans, Aug 2025

"Would matching here preclude from building a compliant future curb ramp?"

— SDOT reviewer comment, 90% grading plans, Aug 2025

The Levy · 2024

What voters approved
and what it funded

When Seattle voters approved the 2024 Transportation Levy in November of that year, they were told they were voting for, among other things, new sidewalks for underserved neighborhoods. What the ballot did not prominently disclose was that a Council amendment in July 2024 — sponsored by Council member Rob Saka — had renamed the levy's pedestrian infrastructure program from "New Sidewalks" to "New Sidewalks and Sidewalk Alternatives."

The amendment added $48 million to the program and revised its delivery metric to include alternatives to concrete sidewalks as legitimate forms of program delivery. The phrase "sidewalk alternatives" now appears throughout the final ordinance as a valid form of levy delivery.

The 21st Ave S asphalt walkway with precast concrete wheel stops — labeled internally by SDOT as "Low Cost Sidewalks" — is precisely that kind of alternative. The levy requires that a minimum of 17% of its sidewalk and alternative blocks be delivered in District 2. The 21st Ave S project counts toward that minimum.

When voters approved new sidewalks for the people who need them most, this is one of the projects they funded.

The community does not dispute that the levy passed with strong voter support. The concern is that when voters approved "new sidewalks," they may not have understood that asphalt walkways on one side of residential streets — in historically underserved neighborhoods — would count as fulfillment of that commitment.

The Debt · 2024

Who gets what —
and why it matters

The 2024 Transportation Levy sets minimum funding floors for sidewalk delivery in three of Seattle's seven council districts. District 5 receives a minimum of 36% — approximately $40 million. District 1 receives 22%. District 2 receives 17% — the lowest named floor of the three, roughly $19 million.

The levy's own whereas clauses explain the District 5 floor: when North Seattle was annexed in 1954, sidewalks were promised but never built, leaving the area with 36% of the city's missing sidewalk gaps. The logic is sound. A government that failed to build infrastructure at the moment of annexation owes that community a debt.

The same logic, applied consistently, would produce a very different result for District 2.

The argument in one sentence

The levy rewards the neighborhood whose sidewalk debt is newer. It underfunds the neighborhood whose sidewalk debt was created by racial exclusion.

The levy ordinance is explicit about why District 5 gets 36%: the 1954 annexation created the gap, and the city acknowledges it owes a debt. But Beacon Hill's families were already here — many of them on these specific blocks — before the District 5 annexation occurred. They were here because racial restrictive covenants and redlining made North Seattle and most of the rest of the city unavailable to them. The University of Washington Civil Rights and Labor History Project documents that North Seattle remained virtually all white through the 1950s, 60s, 70s, and 80s. The 1954 annexees had access to FHA-backed mortgages, new construction, and sidewalks-promised suburban expansion. The families on 21st Ave S did not.

And yet by every metric that should govern infrastructure investment — years waited, pedestrian danger, equity score — District 2 scores higher. SDOT's own collision data shows District 2 accounts for 56% of all fatal and serious pedestrian injury crashes citywide. SDOT's own prioritization dataset rates the missing sidewalks on 21st Ave S and 22nd Ave S at Tier 1 with a perfect equity score of 12 out of 12. The levy minimum for the district that scores highest on danger and equity is less than half the minimum for the district that scores highest on political history.

The 21st Ave S asphalt walkway — $350,000, one side, no community input — counts toward District 2's 17% minimum. By the levy's own accounting, the debt is settled. The community has a different word for it: unfinished. On the ground, there is still no sidewalk.

Outro · May 2026

The open
question

In February 2026, fifty-eight residents of 21st Ave S were surveyed about the proposed plan. Not one preferred it. Eighty-eight percent reported feeling unsafe or very unsafe walking the street today. Twenty-six families said they walk the block with children or strollers. Four residents use wheelchairs or assistive devices. A neighbor named Kashina said her family had decided not to rent on the block because her child would have to walk in the travel lane to reach a school bus.

In November 2025, Dougherty told community organizer Dana Lee by phone that SDOT would not oppose permanent concrete sidewalks if the community escalated to City Council and secured additional resources. He did not offer to facilitate that escalation. He informed them that the burden was theirs to carry — the burden of lobbying and funding the infrastructure that other Seattle neighborhoods received as a matter of course. SDOT's own prioritization dataset rates these blocks Tier 1 with a perfect equity score of 12 out of 12 — the maximum possible. Among 11,550 missing sidewalk segments citywide, this block ranks in the top 7.8%. An asphalt path on one side of the street is not the standard the rest of Seattle is held to. It should not be the finish line here.

Sandi, a neighbor whose family moved to 21st Ave S in 1967, recalled that her father had once tried to self-fund sidewalks on the block and could not get sufficient neighbor buy-in. The mechanism that was supposed to fill that gap — public infrastructure investment, equitably distributed — produced instead a proviso redirected without notice, a scope locked without input, a design with unresolved engineering questions, and an asphalt path on one side of one street.

There is a precedent that worries this community. S Kenyon St in South Beacon Hill received a similar asphalt quick-build in 2021. It is already deteriorating. Wheel stops are being swallowed by weeds. The effective walkway width may fall short of ADA standards. That is the pattern this community is trying to avoid.

The community's ask to CM Eddie Lin is direct: go on record that an asphalt path on one side of the street is not the finish line for this block. The city's long-term obligation is permanent concrete sidewalks on both sides — the same standard every other developed street in Seattle is held to. That means keeping 21st Ave S on the city's infrastructure agenda with a plan and a timeline, not filing it away as complete when the asphalt is poured. And it means requiring SDOT to answer in writing the question their own reviewers asked and never resolved: does this project permanently close the pedestrian infrastructure need for this block?

"Walking this street feels like a matter of when a pedestrian will be hit, not if."

— Letha, resident

What the community is asking for

From CM Eddie Lin: Go on record that an asphalt path on one side is not the finish line. State publicly that this block's long-term obligation is permanent concrete sidewalks on both sides — the standard every other developed street in Seattle is held to — and commit to keeping 21st Ave S on the city's infrastructure agenda with a plan and a timeline.

From SDOT: A written answer to the question their own reviewers asked and never resolved — does this project permanently close the pedestrian infrastructure need for 21st Ave S between S Bayview St and S Walker St? And if not, what does the path to full infrastructure look like?

What equity requires: Permanent concrete sidewalks on both sides. The same infrastructure this community has paid taxes toward for 40–60 years. The same standard other Seattle neighborhoods already have. Not someday. With a plan.
