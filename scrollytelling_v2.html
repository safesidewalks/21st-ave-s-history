Redlining · 1889–1954

“No person other than
the Caucasian race.”

Seattle’s neighborhoods were not shaped by preference. By 1889, property deeds named who could live where — most of the city legally closed to Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, Black, and Jewish families. Beacon Hill was not.

The neighborhoods that were off-limits were being built out in the 1950s with FHA-backed mortgages and promised sidewalks. The families on 21st Ave S received neither. In 1967, Sandi’s family moved here. There was no sidewalk. There had never been one. There still isn’t one today.

Neighbor record · 1967–present

In the decades after her family moved in, Sandi’s father did what the city would not: he tried to organize the neighbors to fund sidewalk construction themselves. He couldn’t get enough buy-in. The sidewalk didn’t get built.

When public infrastructure investment is withheld from communities of color, the burden of filling that gap falls on residents themselves — through self-organized funding drives, advocacy labor, and decades of effort that change nothing. The families on 21st Ave S have paid property taxes for 40 to 60 years. Those taxes funded sidewalks in other Seattle neighborhoods — neighborhoods that were, by legal design, more accessible to white families.

Without a sidewalk · 1967–present

What the gap costs

The covenants are gone. What they produced is not.

When infrastructure investment is withheld from a neighborhood — by law, then by policy, then by inertia — the gap doesn’t stay abstract. It becomes a daily calculation. Which side of the street is safer today. Whether to let your child walk to the bus stop alone. What to do about the water pooling in your driveway because the drainage was never built right.

The families on 21st Ave S have been running those calculations for decades.

In 2015, Lauren borrowed a radar gun from SDOT, collected speed data on 21st Ave S herself — a city requirement before SDOT would conduct its own study — and turned in the results. A year later, having heard nothing, she wrote:

“My results should have triggered an SDOT study. Can you tell me when you will be conducting your follow up study?”

Lauren, neighbor — email to SDOT, March 2016

There is no record anyone followed up. SDOT’s own study came seven years later: 77% of drivers on S College St exceed the 25 mph speed limit. The 85th percentile speed: 32.7 mph.

In February 2020, a Beacon Hill Elementary student and her mother were struck by a truck at 22nd Ave S and S College St. In June 2023, Letha watched a post-collision rescue extrication at the same intersection and emailed SDOT that day. The following month, multiple collisions were reported at the same intersection.

District 2 accounts for 56% of all fatal and serious pedestrian crashes in Seattle.

Meanwhile, neighbors have managed their own drainage for decades. In the 1990s, several households pooled their own money to build asphalt berms to keep stormwater from flooding their homes — a problem that had gone unaddressed for years. A resident who has lived on the block since the 1980s still remembers it. Calvin, whose parents owned the home before him, still deals with uncontrolled runoff from a city storm drain in front of the property today.
